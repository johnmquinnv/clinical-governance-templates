# Ukraine's civilian-military medical interface, 2014-2026

**Author:** John Quinn, MD, MPH, PhD, EMT-P  
**Version:** 0.1.0 · **Evidence checked:** 31 October 2026  
**Status:** public-source educational analysis; independent review pending.

## The problem

Ukraine's experience shows why medical interoperability must connect the full continuum of care: community access, prehospital response, resuscitation, surgery, critical care, rehabilitation and return to a functioning civilian service. A transfer depends on more than an available vehicle. The sending team, transport provider, receiving service and coordinating authorities need compatible information, clear acceptance and agreed responsibilities.

This case examines how public accounts of humanitarian coordination, medical evacuation, workforce development and NATO-Ukraine learning can inform a practical educational project. It uses selected primary sources rather than operational records. It does not establish a complete history or a causal effect on survival.

## Evolution documented in public sources

| Period | Documented development | Interface exposed by the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 2014-2021 | WHO describes an emergency health response beginning in 2014, with disrupted services, displacement and humanitarian medical assistance. [1] | Continuity of civilian care and coordination across disrupted services predate the full-scale invasion. |
| 2022 | The European Commission launched its evacuation scheme in March and opened the EU Medevac Hub near Rzeszow on September 1. Its account identifies Ukraine/Poland cooperation, receiving countries, nursing support and input from WHO and IOM. [2] | Patient movement crosses clinical, administrative and national boundaries; it requires an accepting destination and information transfer. |
| 2024 | WHO reported a national emergency-care training rollout with physicians, ambulance workers and nurses, including development of national instructors. [3] | Workforce development must connect professional groups and local teaching capacity. |
| 2025 | NATO and Ukraine opened JATEC in Bydgoszcz on February 17 to analyze and apply military-civilian lessons. A May medical workshop examined war-wound infections, antimicrobial resistance and treatment process maps. [4,5] | A structured learning mechanism can connect experience to policy, training and process improvement. |
| 2026 | WHO's February appeal includes trauma care, workforce resilience, continuity of essential care and medical evacuation aligned with the Ministry of Health and international partners. [6] | Evacuation and preparedness remain part of a wider health-system response. |

These are overlapping developments, not a claim that one institution replaced another or that the system progressed uniformly. The sources describe different mandates and populations. They do not provide a single linked dataset spanning the whole period.

## A worked interface: movement through an international evacuation hub

The Commission's 2022 hub description provides a bounded example. A patient arriving from Ukraine could receive interim nursing and other support before onward travel to treatment in another participating country. The description also identifies an established evacuation procedure and a secure mechanism for sharing health records. [2] That is evidence of a coordination arrangement, not proof that every handover was complete or every patient reached care without delay.

The following map is an **analytical reconstruction for teaching**, not the operational EU or Ukrainian procedure:

| Interface | Question a coordination exercise should test | Observable evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Sending service to coordinator | What care is needed, who is requesting support and what is unresolved? | A clear request, named accountable role and a list of missing information |
| Coordinator to receiving service | Has the destination accepted the request and its relevant requirements? | Explicit acceptance, contact role and documented conditions |
| Clinical team to transport provider | Has the responsible team confirmed the proposed transfer arrangements? | Recorded review and ownership of outstanding requirements |
| Transport or hub to receiving team | Is essential information understandable and responsibility transferred? | Structured handover and receiving acknowledgment |
| Receiving service to coordinating network | What happened, what remains open and who follows it up? | Feedback, action owner and closure evidence |

The exercise should test coordination and documentation using fictional cases. Clinical eligibility, consent, lawful information sharing, staffing and transport authorization must come from the applicable authorities and clinical framework. Humanitarian organizations retain their own mandates; coordination does not place them under military command.

## What training evidence adds

A published narrative evaluation of the CoROM/Tactical Medicine North DCR-Ukraine program reports 104 course completions across three iterations, curriculum adaptation and increasing Ukrainian instructor leadership. [7] It provides a documented example of iteration and localization. Its design does not establish a causal reduction in mortality, and completions are not unique-person or patient-outcome counts.

**Analysis:** the useful educational connection is to combine clinical-content expertise with observable system behaviors: clarify responsibility, communicate across professional and language boundaries, manage a failed communication channel and record an improvement action. The existence of a training program and a coordination mechanism does not, by itself, show that they are integrated. That relationship needs to be tested and documented.

## A proposed contribution from CoROM and DHI

The following is a proposal for discussion, subject to the organizations' agreement. It does not announce an institutional partnership or an approved course.

1. **Map one interface with Ukrainian partners.** Select a bounded, publicly discussable problem, such as receiving acceptance or follow-up after transfer. Record the intended users, responsibilities, terms, constraints and existing tools before designing another product.
2. **Build a joint teaching exercise.** Use CoROM's relevant educational expertise and DHI's disaster-health and evaluation interests to develop a curriculum map, fictional scenario, facilitator guide and observation rubric. Start with coordination; place any future DCP clinical content through a separate curriculum and governance review.
3. **Pilot, revise and report.** Use the [pilot protocol](https://github.com/johnmquinnv/medical-education-resources/blob/main/training-design/pilot-protocol.md) to document usability, language needs, assessor interpretation and revision decisions. Invite independent Ukrainian clinical and methodological review before wider use.
4. **Return a traceable learning product.** Produce a short report connecting the observed problem, proposed change, test and result. Use the relevant receiving organization's process for any submission to JATEC, MILMED COE or another partner. Do not label a proposal as adopted NATO policy.

## Measures and limits

Candidate measures for a fictional exercise include the proportion of applicable handovers with explicit acceptance, the proportion of unresolved actions with an owner, and completion of the agreed feedback loop. Define each denominator and its applicability before observation. Also record time burden, interpretation disagreements and unintended duplication of work as balancing measures. Do not infer patient safety from documentation completeness alone.

Key evidence gaps include linked patient outcomes, regional variation, durable behavior change after training and the experience of receiving civilian services. This selected-source review cannot resolve those gaps. A future evaluation would need an appropriate design, lawful access, independent oversight and a clear distinction between research and service improvement.
